Patagonia day-hiking plans

Five ways for two people to spend 25 days hiking from Balmaceda, sleeping in the car. Every plan uses only day hikes on marked, non-technical trails (no alpine routes or exposed passes). Guided trips are left out, apart from two short optional extras I think are really worth it. Each plan keeps a few flex days for bad weather, because wind decides which days you can hike. Every plan page has an interactive map with the driving route and the trails, plus a GPX file to load into OsmAnd, Maps.me or Gaia GPS for offline use. Each plan page also has a "Show GPT options" switch that adds extra hikes from the Greater Patagonian Trail manual (in purple, without changing the plan). The scouting reports behind them are in gpt-reports.

My recommendation for you

Best fit: Plan 3, Aysén + El Chaltén

You have the Argentina permit, you can hike back to back, and you sleep in the car. That makes Plan 3 the best match: it has the most top-quality, safe, non-technical day hikes, and it costs only about €720 per person. El Chaltén trails start in town and need no guide. Plan 4 adds Torres del Paine and Perito Moreno. It still fits your budget at about €900 per person, but it has fewer hiking days and many more hours behind the wheel.

Cheapest and most relaxed: Plan 2

If you would rather keep money in reserve and drive less, Plan 2 stays in Chile with long stays at the best hiking bases. Plan 1 is for the full road-trip experience, and Plan 5 for volcanoes and rainforest.

Staying safe without alpine terrain

Weather is the real danger

  • Wind gusts of 80–100 km/h on ridges and moraines are normal. Check windy.com, meteoblue and mountain-forecast.com every evening, and match the big hikes to the calm days (that is what the buffer days are for).
  • Set a turnaround time and keep to it. Summer days are long (sunset around 21:30), but storms arrive quickly.
  • Rivers rise in the afternoon when the snow melts. Never ford a river on foot that comes above your knees.

Logistics that matter

  • Car: a high-clearance 4x4 or pickup, ideally with 2 spare tyres. Most of the Carretera south of Cerro Castillo is gravel.
  • Sleeping in the car: nights are 0–8 °C and windy. Bring sleeping bags rated around 0 °C, window covers for privacy and against condensation, and park with the nose into the wind. Paid campgrounds give you showers and a sheltered place to cook. Use them every 2–3 nights.
  • Fuel: fill up whenever you can. Stations are far apart (Villa O'Higgins, Tortel and Ruta 40 especially). A jerrycan is worth having.
  • No signal on most trails and long stretches of road. Download offline maps (Maps.me / OsmAnd / Wikiloc) and consider a satellite messenger (inReach or similar).
  • Register at CONAF ranger stations where asked (Cerro Castillo has a latest start time).
  • Cash: many small places take cash only, and ATMs run out at weekends.

Book now (January and February are peak season)

  • The rental car (4x4, plus the Argentina permit if needed)
  • Santiago ⇄ Balmaceda flights (for the return, fly on Feb 21, not on the 22nd)
  • The Chile Chico ⇄ Puerto Ibáñez car ferry (Somarco)
  • Park tickets online: pasesparques.cl (Cerro Castillo, Queulat, Reserva Coyhaique, Torres del Paine; buy before you lose signal) and ventaweb.apn.gob.ar (Los Glaciares, card only)
  • Torres del Paine park tickets (online only)

Pack

  • A properly waterproof jacket and overtrousers, a warm layer, a hat and gloves, even in February
  • Trekking poles (for steep moraines), and boots with good grip
  • Sunscreen and sunglasses: the UV is strong under the ozone hole
  • Insect repellent and long sleeves: tábanos (horseflies) are fierce in Aysén in January
  • A water filter, a stove, a power bank and a car charger
